# Testimonial requests — ready to send

**Why this is the highest-value thing left.** The site is finished: twelve case
studies, three products, five concepts, a free demo, two working forms, five
languages. What it does not have is one sentence from another human being saying
you were good to work with. A stranger with a $9,000 budget has no reason to
believe you yet — and no copy I write fixes that, because the problem isn't what
is said, it's *who says it*.

Two real quotes will move your conversion rate more than everything built today
combined.

---

## Before you send

**Send three. Expect two.** People are busy and mean well. Three asks gets you
roughly two replies, which is exactly what you need — the section looks
established at two, thin at one.

**Send them Monday to Wednesday morning.** Not Thursday, not the weekend.

**One follow-up only,** seven days later, then let it go. Chasing a favour
damages the relationship that made the favour possible.

**Offer to draft it.** Most people want to say yes and don't want to write. The
draft is the difference between a 30% and an 80% reply rate. There is a template
for that at the bottom.

## If they say "can you draft it?"

Say yes immediately — this is the good outcome, not a burden.

**Write it in their voice, not yours.** Modest, specific, and slightly
understated. A testimonial that sounds like marketing copy reads as fake, even
when it isn't.

**Template:**

> "We came to Ahmed with [specific problem]. He [specific thing he did], and
> [specific outcome]. What I'd say to anyone considering him is [one honest,
> slightly unexpected thing — 'he tells you when an idea is bad', 'he handed
> over documentation we actually use']."

**The last clause is what makes it believable.** Uniformly glowing quotes read
as invented. A small, specific, mildly critical detail is what makes a reader
trust the rest.

Send it with: *"Here's a draft — please change anything, or bin it entirely and
write your own. I won't be offended."* And mean it.

---

## The line I won't cross

**Do not publish anything the named person hasn't approved.** Not a paraphrase,
not "something they said once on a call", not a quote you're confident they'd
agree with.

Drafting for someone to approve is normal and honest — they sign off, so the
words become theirs. Inventing a quote and attributing it to a real business is
fabrication, and if it's ever discovered it destroys every true thing on the
site along with it. You have twelve real case studies. You don't need to invent
anything.
